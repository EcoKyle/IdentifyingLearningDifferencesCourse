Recognizing Patterns Without Labels

Understanding learning differences doesn't require a diagnosis. In fact, many students who struggle with organization, reading, or attention never receive a formal label—but that doesn't mean their needs should go unnoticed.

As educators, we can learn to recognize patterns in how students engage, communicate, and persist with tasks. These patterns—especially when observed over time—can offer important clues about how a student learns best, and where they may need support.

For example, a student who often forgets materials, misses deadlines, or has trouble planning out assignments might be showing signs of executive function challenges. These brain-based skills help us manage time, focus attention, and remember instructions. Patrick (2022) encourages teachers to watch for small signals like inconsistent performance, frustration with long-term projects, or avoiding multi-step tasks—then respond with proactive supports like visual checklists, structured routines, or breaking assignments into steps.

Similarly, a student who struggles to sound out words, guesses when reading, or avoids reading aloud might be showing signs of decoding difficulties. These are common among students with dyslexia or other reading-based learning differences. According to the International Dyslexia Association (2017), signs may include poor spelling, slow reading, or difficulty following written directions. But these students often have strong listening comprehension and creative thinking—strengths that can be missed if we only focus on what they can't do yet.

Key Learning Terms

Executive Function

Mental processes that help us plan, focus, remember, and manage tasks. These are skills we can build, not traits that are fixed.

Working Memory

The ability to hold and manipulate information in the mind (e.g., remembering multi-step directions). Students may understand the task but lose steps along the way.

Decoding

The ability to sound out words based on letter patterns and sounds. A challenge with decoding doesn't mean low intelligence—it means the brain processes written language differently.

Processing Speed

How quickly a student can take in and respond to information. A slower pace doesn't mean a lack of understanding—it may just mean they need more time.

Flexible Thinking

The ability to shift perspectives or adapt to change. Students who struggle here might get “stuck” on one way of doing things.

Self-Regulation

The ability to manage emotions, attention, and behavior. Students may need support learning how to pause, ask for help, or stay calm during challenges.

Sensory Processing

How the brain receives and responds to sights, sounds, textures, and movement. Some students may seek out or avoid certain sensory input, which can affect focus and comfort in class.

Task Initiation

The ability to start a task without delay. Students who hesitate may seem unmotivated, but often they're unsure where to begin or feel overwhelmed.

Attention Shifting

Switching focus from one activity or idea to another. Students who struggle here may need extra support with transitions or adapting to changes.

Learning Profile

A broad view of how a student learns best, including their strengths, needs, and preferred supports. Learning profiles help guide more personalized teaching.
