Telling the Full Story

By this point, you've practiced observing student behaviors, identifying patterns, and communicating with students and families in ways that center respect and possibility. Now, it's time to bring all of that together.

When we talk about a student's learning profile—whether in a meeting, a summary, or an email—we're not just describing a learner. We're shaping how others see that learner.

That's why it's so important to tell a full story: one that includes strengths, struggles, context, and care.

Let's look at two examples that describe the same student:

  • ❌ Deficit-Based Description:
    “Cho struggles with focus and disrupts class often. She has difficulty staying in her seat and rarely finishes independent work. She is easily distracted and requires constant redirection.”
  • ✅ Strengths-Based, Holistic Description:
    “Cho is a highly verbal student who thrives in discussion and hands-on learning. She shows persistence when engaged with tasks that involve movement or collaboration. Cho benefits from short, clear instructions and opportunities to check in with a trusted adult. She is working on building sustained attention during independent work and has shown growth when routines are consistent.”

The difference isn't just tone—it's usefulness.

The first version limits what we believe about Cho. The second opens the door to collaboration, support, and progress. It gives other educators (and Cho herself) tools to understand how she learns, not just where she struggles.

When we document student profiles using:

  • Specific observations (what we saw, not just what we assumed),
  • Clear strengths (skills, interests, or moments of success),
  • Supportive needs (what helps, when, and how),

—we help everyone involved in a student's journey respond with clarity and compassion.

This kind of narrative also honors the student's humanity. It says: You are more than your challenges. You are a learner with potential—and we see you.

And when we write this way, families feel more invited into the process. Colleagues are more likely to build from what's already working. And students? They begin to believe the story themselves.
