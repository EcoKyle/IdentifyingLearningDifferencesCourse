Building Trust with Families

Talking with families about a student's struggles can feel intimidating. Many teachers worry about saying the wrong thing, causing offense, or opening a conversation they don't know how to finish. But families don't expect perfection—they want partnership.

As Kaufman et al. (2021) explain, when families and educators communicate openly and respectfully, students benefit. But for that trust to grow, families must feel like valued members of the team, not outsiders receiving a report.

One of the most effective ways to build this trust is to begin with strengths.

Starting a conversation with, “Let me tell you what your child does really well,” shifts the tone immediately. It shows families that you see their child as more than a challenge—that you recognize their growth, interests, and contributions. It also builds a bridge to discussing support needs without shame or defensiveness.

  • Begin with a strength: Lead with something genuine that the student does well—academically, socially, or behaviorally.
  • Ask before telling: Questions like, “What have you noticed at home?” or “What has worked well for your child in the past?” show respect for family knowledge.
  • Use plain language: Avoid jargon or clinical terms unless the family uses them first. Say “support for reading” instead of “phonological processing deficit.”
  • Find out how families prefer to communicate: Some may prefer text or in-person chats over email or formal meetings.
  • Stay curious, not conclusive: Share what you've observed, not just what you think it means.

It's also important to recognize that some families—especially those from marginalized communities—may have had negative experiences with schools in the past. They might expect to be blamed or ignored. By listening actively and affirming that you're working with them, you can begin to repair and rebuild that trust.

Here's a phrase that can help:
“You know your child best. I'd love to hear what's worked for them before and what you'd like to see going forward.”

Even brief, positive interactions—like a quick call home to share a win—can make future conversations about learning needs feel more collaborative and less confrontational.
