The Power of Observation

When a student is struggling, our first instinct is often to solve the problem or offer support. But before we can help, we need to observe—closely, consistently, and without assumptions.

Observation is one of the most powerful tools a teacher has. It helps us notice not just when a student is off-task or confused, but how they approach learning, where they get stuck, and what sparks their success. And when documented over time, those observations can reveal patterns we might otherwise miss.

But not all observation is equally useful.

According to Davis & Southward (2019), educators should aim for structured, objective observations—notes that describe what a student does, not what we think it means. For example:

  • ✅ “The student looked around the room for 10 seconds before starting the task.”
  • 🚫 Not: “The student was confused and unmotivated.”

The difference matters. One describes a behavior. The other jumps to a conclusion.

Objective observation helps us avoid bias—especially the unconscious kind. Without realizing it, we might interpret behavior differently based on a student's tone, identity, or prior history. But when we write down only what we see and hear, we stay grounded in facts. That gives us better data for support teams, parent conversations, and our own instructional decisions.

Tip: Try these prompts when observing students:

  • “What did I see or hear?”
  • “How often did it happen?”
  • “What was happening around the student at the time?”

The goal isn't to catch mistakes—it's to build a clearer picture of how a student learns and interacts over time.
