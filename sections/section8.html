Focusing on What's Working

It's easy to focus on what's not going well—missed homework, off-task behavior, low test scores. But what if the first thing we noticed was what is working?

When documenting student learning, beginning with strengths doesn't mean ignoring challenges. It means anchoring our understanding in a more complete, hopeful picture of who the student is—not just where they struggle, but where they succeed.

Loftin (2022) explains that strengths-based language does more than sound positive—it actually changes outcomes. Students feel more motivated when they see their abilities named and valued. Families are more engaged when they hear what their child is doing well. And educators are more likely to set effective, meaningful goals when those goals build on real skills and interests.

Both describe the same student. But strengths-based language tells us what works—and that's where solutions begin.

When documenting, try to include:

  • What the student does well (e.g., strong verbal skills, visual learning, leadership in group settings)
  • What helps them succeed (e.g., flexible formats, movement breaks, modeling)
  • What challenges still need support (e.g., initiating tasks, decoding unfamiliar words)

Capturing both needs and assets helps offer a more accurate and empowering student profile—one that helps teachers, support staff, and families work together with clarity and compassion.

And perhaps most importantly, we help students see themselves as capable learners who have something valuable to contribute.
