What Do We Mean by “Learning Differences?”

Every student brings a unique way of thinking, feeling, and learning into the classroom. Some students process written instructions quickly, while others might need to hear something aloud or see it in action. Some thrive in group discussions, while others think best when working solo. These differences aren't problems—they're part of what makes a learning environment rich and full of potential.

When we talk about learning differences, we're referring to the many natural ways students take in, make sense of, and express what they know. These can include differences in reading, attention, memory, sensory processing, executive functioning, and social communication. Rather than seeing these as deficits, we can understand them as variations in how the brain works—something researchers and educators call neurodiversity.

Neurodiversity is the idea that there's no single “right” way to think or learn. It reminds us that traits like ADHD, autism, and dyslexia are examples of how the brain can function differently—not incorrectly. As Woods (2025) puts it, neurodiversity-affirming classrooms recognize that neurological differences are part of human diversity, and they focus on adjusting the environment—not the student.

This shift in perspective is key. Instead of asking, "How do I fix this student's behavior or learning challenge?" we ask, "How can I create space for this student's strengths to shine?" When we begin from a place of curiosity and respect, we're more likely to notice what a student can do—even if they do it differently than their peers.

In practice, this might mean giving a student with dyslexia access to audio books so they can engage with content at their intellectual level, or offering a student with ADHD choices in how they complete a task so they can stay motivated and focused. It's not about lowering expectations—it's about removing unnecessary barriers so all students can thrive (Skelling, 2019).

Embracing neurodiversity and adopting a strengths-based mindset helps us move closer to inclusive teaching. We begin to see learning differences not as obstacles to manage, but as unique learning profiles to understand and support.
