Speaking with Students About Supports

At some point, many students begin to notice when they receive accommodations that others don't—extra time on a test, a quieter space to work, or reminders from a teacher. They might ask, “Why do I get that?” or even wonder, “Is something wrong with me?”

These questions are natural—and how we answer them can make a lasting impact.

According to Saini (2025), the best way to talk about supports is with openness, honesty, and a sense of normalcy. When we present accommodations as tools—not signs of weakness—we help students understand that getting what they need is not only okay, it's fair.

One powerful way to explain this is through metaphor. For example, a common one used with younger students is the stool story:

If three students are trying to reach a high shelf, and one is shorter than the others, we give them a stool. That's not unfair—it's what helps everyone reach the same goal.

This kind of comparison helps students see that fairness doesn't always mean “the same.” It means everyone getting the support they need to succeed.

With older students, conversations can shift toward self-advocacy—helping them understand what supports work for them, and why. Teens are often more receptive when they're treated as partners in the process, not passive recipients. Saini recommends involving students in the planning of how supports are introduced, framed, and adjusted.

Here are some conversation starters that educators can use:

  • “Have you noticed when learning feels easier for you? Let's talk about what's helping.”
  • “Lots of students benefit from extra time or quiet space. You're not alone.”
  • “You're not getting special treatment—you're getting what helps you show what you know.”

It's also important to respect students' privacy and readiness. If a student isn't aware of their full learning profile—or is uncomfortable with it—it's okay to keep explanations simple and focused on tools, not labels.

When we talk about supports with care and clarity, we don't just inform students—we empower them. They begin to see that needing help isn't a flaw. It's a part of learning that many people experience. And it's something they're allowed to understand, ask about, and even shape for themselves.
