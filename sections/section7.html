From Problems to Patterns

Once we've collected objective observations, the next step is to look for patterns—not just isolated behaviors, but consistent signals that tell us when, where, and how a student thrives or struggles.

Patterns can reveal a lot. A student might have difficulty focusing right after lunch but engage fully in morning activities. Another might avoid writing tasks in every subject, but contribute rich ideas during class discussions. These patterns help us shift our focus from what's wrong to what's happening, when, and why.

To uncover patterns, we ask:

  • Does the behavior happen at a specific time of day?
  • Is it tied to a certain subject, setting, or type of task?
  • What else is going on around the student at that time?
  • Are there moments when the student is engaged and successful?

But there's something else we must consider: context.

Morin (n.d.) reminds us that behavior often communicates an unmet need—but the meaning of that behavior can vary depending on the student's experiences, background, and environment. What looks like avoidance might actually be anxiety. What seems like defiance could be a sign of frustration or sensory overload.

That's why it's important to observe without judgment, and with cultural responsiveness in mind.

Kinda (2021) urges educators to consider how our own beliefs or expectations might color how we interpret behavior. For example, a student who avoids eye contact might be seen as “disrespectful” in one classroom, but in their cultural context, they're showing politeness or shyness. A quiet student might not be disengaged—they might be taught to listen more than speak.

The more we learn about our students' identities, strengths, and experiences, the more accurately we can interpret what we see—and avoid mislabeling behaviors.

Observations are most helpful when we treat them as pieces of a puzzle. No single note tells the whole story. But over time, patterns begin to emerge—and those patterns point us toward the kinds of support that will help a student succeed.

In the next section, we'll shift our lens even further—from noticing what's challenging to noticing what's already working. Every student brings strengths into the classroom. The better we can identify and document those strengths, the more empowered our support plans will be.
