Behavior as a Window into Needs

Sometimes, the most important things students are trying to tell us never come out in words. Instead, they show up as restlessness, silence, outbursts, or even refusal to participate.

In classrooms, it's easy to see behavior as a disruption. But what if we looked at it differently?

Many educators and researchers now embrace the idea that behavior is communication. When a student pushes away a worksheet, walks out of class, or refuses to join a group, they may be telling us something—I'm overwhelmed, I'm confused, or I don't feel safe.

According to Morin (n.d.), behavior can often signal an unmet need or a lagging skill. These might include:

  • A need for clarity (e.g., not understanding directions)
  • A sensory need (e.g., overwhelmed by noise or lighting)
  • A need for connection (e.g., feeling isolated or misunderstood)
  • A lack of self-regulation skills (e.g., difficulty calming down after frustration)

Autism Speaks (n.d.) emphasizes that many autistic students—and others with communication differences—may express themselves through nontraditional behaviors, especially in environments that aren't designed with them in mind. When we assume the behavior is “the problem,” we risk missing the deeper story.

Instead of asking, “How do I stop this behavior?” we can start with:

  • “When does this behavior usually happen?”
  • “What happened right before?”
  • “What might the student be reacting to?”
  • “What support haven't we tried yet?”

This shift doesn't mean ignoring behavior. It means responding to it with curiosity and care, not punishment or shame. When students feel seen, they're more likely to trust us—and that trust opens the door to learning.

You don't need to know a student's diagnosis to respond compassionately. You just need to be willing to look at their behavior as a clue—not a conclusion.
